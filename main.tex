\section{Introduction}

This paper will try to solve the Pentomino Pathfinding problem for various rectangular grids. See \cite{v1} and \cite{v2} for further introduction.

The Pentomino Pathfinding problem is as follows:

\begin{tcolorbox}[
    sharp corners,
    toprule=0pt,
    bottomrule=0pt,
    rightrule=0pt,
    halign=justify,
    colback=blue!5!white,
    colframe=blue!20!white,
]
For a rectangular $n \times m$ grid of \hspace{-0.04em}\newterm{squares} or \hspace{-0.12em}\newterm{cells}, place a subset of the twelve pentominoes (\cref{fig:pentominoes}) on the grid \techdetail{without overlapping}.

\begin{definition}[Empty]
\techdetail{A square is \newterm{empty} without a pentomino.}\marginnote{Pentominoes are like walls.}
\end{definition}

Now that we have some placements $p$, empty squares $A$ and $B$ are then automatically chosen such that $\#^{p}_{n, m}$ = the length of (the shortest path from $A$ to $B$) is maximized.

\begin{definition}[Path]
A \newterm{path} is an orthogonally-connected \minordetail{nonempty} sequence of empty cells.
In particular, ``\newterm{the path}" is the above automatically chosen path, where the placements and grid size come from context.
\end{definition}

Let $\#_{n,m}$ be the maximum $\#^{p}_{n,m}$ for all placements $p$.

Find $\#_{n,m}$.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{definition}[$\#$]
$\#(s)$ is the size of a set or the length of a path or sequence. In addition, $\#(\thepath)$ given some placements $p$ is denoted $\#^{p}_{n, m}$. And $\#_{n,m}$ is the maximum $\#^{p}_{n,m}$ for all placements $p$. Finally, \highlight{the shorthand $\#_n$ refers to $\#_{n, n}$.}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Solution]
Consider some placements $p$, i.e., consider some placements of a subset of the twelve pentominoes in the grid whose size ($n \times m$) is given by context. Then $p$ is a valid \newterm{solution} if and only if $\#^p_{n,m} = \#_{n,m}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Adjacency]
Two squares are \newterm{adjacent} iff they are \newterm{diagonal} or \newterm{orthogonal} (\cref{fig:adj}).\footnote{In technical terms, squares are \newterm{orthogonal} when they have a Manhattan distance of 1, \newterm{adjacent} when they have a Chebyshev distance of 1, and \newterm{diagonal} when they are adjacent but not orthogonal, for distances ignoring nonempty cells.} Two sets of squares $S$ and $T$ \hspace{-0.05em}are \newterm{adjacent} iff there are two squares $s \in S$, $t \in T$ where $s$ and $t$ are adjacent \techdetail{(an individual square $s$ is the same as its singleton set \hspace{-0.05em}$\{s\}$\hspace{-0.05em} for this purpose)}. The squares adjacent to a set of squares $S$ is denoted $\adj(S)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Subgrid]
\techdetail{A \newterm{subgrid} is a set of orthogonally adjacent squares.}\footnote{That is, a connected subgraph. \minordetail{(All squares are in some given or assumed given grid).}}\marginnote{\badterm{Wrong?}: it is the counterpart to a platter}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Brimming]
\label{def:brimming}

Given some placements $p$, the grid is \newterm{brimming} when every empty square is in the path. In other words, all squares are `used': part of a pentomino or the path.
\end{definition}

\begin{property}\label{prop:brimline}
In a brimming grid, the empty squares form the path. They are isomorphic to a straight line. In particular, neither a \newterm{fork} (an empty cell orthogonally adjacent to three other empty cells) nor a \newterm{cycle} (where the path or subpath doesn't have two ends but zero as the two ends connect) (aka a \newterm{loop}) exist.
\end{property}

\begin{property}
Given a brimming $n \times m$ grid, we have $\#^{p}_{n, m}$ = $nm - 5 \cdot \#(p)$ where $\#(p)$ upper bounds the optimal number of pentominoes by \cref{prop:brimmax}.
\end{property}

In the future, \cref{prop:brimmax} shall implicitly and automatically be used to show that
a solution either cannot fit more pentominoes or is tied with the possibility of
adding one pentomino. Upon showing such a solution with $n$ pentominoes,
it only remains to show that using less than $n$ pentominoes is not better.
This will be implicitly done by the structure of this paper: we shall show the best results
using 0 pentominoes, 1 pentomino, 2 pentominoes, etc., even though these results are inefficient:

\begin{property}[Zero pentomino result]
\label{prop:zeroplace}

With nothing placed, we have $\#^{\emptyset}_{n, m} = n + m - 1$
\end{property}

\begin{property}[One-pentomino solution]
\label{prop:oneplace}

Say we place a pentomino and $n + m - 1, nm - 10 < \#^p_{n, m}$. Then any solution must contain one pentomino.
\end{property}

\begin{definition}[Edge]
\label{def:edge}

\emph{The} \newterm{edge} of a subgrid is the sub-subgrid of squares adjacent to less than 8 squares of the subgrid.
Alternatively, \emph{an} \newterm{edge} of a rectangular $n \times m$ subgrid is an $n \times 1$ or $1 \times m$ sub-subgrid \minordetail{completely} inside the edge of the subgrid.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Corner]
The \newterm{corners} of a rectangular subgrid are the squares in two of its edges.
\end{definition}

\begin{note}
By default, ``edge'' and ``corners'' respectively refer to the edge and corners of the entire grid.
\end{note}

\begin{definition}[Outside]
For a platter $P$, $\outside(P)$ = All squares with a path to the edge if there were no other platters.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Border]
A platter's \newterm{border} is the squares adjacent to the platter that are outside of it. $\border(P) = \adj(P) \cap \outside(P)$
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Border removal]
\label{lem:Border removal}

Say we have a platter $p_1$ with the following property:

\begin{itemize}
\item for any two squares $A$ and $B$ from $\adj(p_1)$ where there is a path from $A$ to $B$, the shortest such path and the shortest such path upon removing the platter are the same length.
\end{itemize}

Then the platter may be removed: $\#^{p}_{n, m} \le \#^{p - p_1}_{n, m}$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The goal is to show that preexisting paths do not get shorter. Without loss of generality, consider a preexisting path that starts and ends outside of the platter. Can it become shorter by entering the platter? No. If the path wants to enter the platter, it must reach its border, go inside, then exit from another square on the border. But by the given property, going \emph{along} the border is at least as efficient.

So, any preexisting paths determining $\#^{p}_{n, m}$ will never enter the area where the platter disappeared from; i.e, such paths will not get shorter with the removal of the platter.

\minordetail{(This logic is then applied to paths which start and end inside the platter. Finally, paths moving between inside and outside stop existing.)}
\end{proof}

\begin{note}
One way to show $\#^{p}_{n, m} < \#^{p - p_1}_{n, m}$ is possible is with a platter taking up every square. In essence, new paths that are longer than the preexisting paths may appear after removing a platter.
\end{note}

\begin{theorem}[Staircase removal]
\label{th:Staircase removal}

A platter $P$ is made of the set of squares $S$ and all squares to the bottom-left of any square in $S$. Then it satisfies \cref{lem:Border removal}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Informally, $\adj(P)$ forms a staircase-like shape. So any path along $\adj(P)$ will go in only two cardinal directions and have length equal to the Manhattan distance between its two endpoints, which cannot be improved upon.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Double staircase removal]
\label{th:Double staircase removal}

A platter $P$ is made of the set of squares $S$ and all squares to the bottom-left of any square in $S$, plus the set of squares $T$ and all squares to the upper-left of any square in $T$. Then it satisfies \cref{lem:Border removal}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Informally, a staircase interrupting another looks like a sideways pyramid cutout, so it still preserves the property. Note that the staircases are defined such that they ``\badterm{begin}" at an edge.
\end{proof}

\begin{note}
This construction may be rotated and reflected.
\end{note}

\begin{theorem}[Sided removal]
\label{th: Sided removal}

This extends \cref{lem:Border removal} to potentially apply to each ``\badterm{side}'' (i.e.\ $\adj(P) \cap \mathrm{subgrid}$) of a platter that cuts the grid into subgrids.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For each subgrid $s$, try applying \cref{th:Staircase removal} as if all the other subgrids were part of the platter. Including these other subgrids does not affect $\adj(P) \cap s$, so this is valid for determining whether a path in $s$ can be improved.
\end{proof}

\begin{note}
If this works for every side of the platter (i.e.\ the platter's entire border) then as a result the whole platter can be removed, since no path within any of the subgrids can be improved.
\end{note}

\begin{corollary}
For $3 \times n$ grids, this removes the vertical orientation of \pentP, two orientations of \pentT, the vertical orientation of \pentU, and all orientations of \pentV, \pentW, and \pentZ{} from consideration.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$2 \times n$}{2 \texttimes\ n} grids}

Consider a $2 \times n$ grid. The only pentominoes that fit are \pentI, \pentL, \pentP, \pentN, \pentU, and \pentY.

By \cref{th: Sided removal}, \pentL, \pentP, \pentN, \pentU, and \pentY{} can be removed from consideration in $2 \times n$ areas, so only \pentI{} is left. While placing \pentI{} on the corner does not help, placing \pentI{} anywhere else increases $\#^p_{2, n}$ by 1 --- so $\#_{2, n} = n + 2$ for $n \ge 7$.

\begin{property}[Cycle inefficiency]
\label{prop:cycineff}

If there is a cycle of empty squares in a grid, the path will not use all empty squares.
\end{property}

\begin{theorem}
\label{th:brimming1u}

When a solution is brimming with only one pentomino, that pentomino is not \pentU.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{prop:brimline}, there are no empty cells adjacent to three other cells.

\pentU{} has a cave square. If the cave square is up against the edge, it is alone, and cannot be in the path because we already covered solutions where $\#(\text{the path}) = 1$.

So the \pentU{} must take a step back. But then the cell adjacent to the cave square is adjacent to at least three cells.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:brimming1}

When a solution is brimming with only one pentomino, the pentomino must include the center of any ($3 \times 3$)-subgrid.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Say the center square is not part of the pentomino. Then the only pentominoes that fit are \pentU{} and \pentV{}. (\cref{fig:brimming1})

But \pentV{} does not cover all the empty $2 \times 2$ cycles, and \pentU{} is not valid by \cref{th:brimming1u}.

If less than 5 squares in the $3 \times 3$ are nonempty, there would be even less covering than from a \pentU{} or \pentV{}.

\begin{property}\label{prop:loopbreaker}
If the path is a subset of the squares in a cycle, then that cycle must be interrupted by orthogonally adjacent minos. (That is, the minos are contiguous). Otherwise, the cycle would be broken up into two different subgrids or more, which is not brimming.
\end{property}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:brimming1}

\cref{lem:brimming1} is equivalent to saying all non-edge squares are part of the pentomino. Additionally, the minos on the edge must be orthogonally adjacent by \cref{prop:loopbreaker}, since the edge of the grid is a loop.
\end{corollary}

$\#_{3, 4} = 7, \#_{3, 5} = 10, \#_{3, 6} = 13, \#_4 = 11$
\end{theorem}

Therefore \cref{cor:brimming1} applies, and it turns out all possibilities with a pentomino containing all non-edge squares and some orthogonally-adjacent edge squares work out.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Latter construction]\label{def:3814lc}
A temporary definition. This refers to a solution with two empty columns on both sides of the grid, instead of 1 + 3 empty columns.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
The cells with the two endpoints are also known as the two \newterm{ends} of the path.
Additionally, the path may \newterm{end} in some subgrid. A subgrid is a \newterm{dead end} if a path cannot enter it without ending there.
\end{definition}

\begin{property}[Cave dead end] \label{prop:cavedend}
Caves are dead ends.
\end{property}

\begin{theorem}[Double emptiness dead end] \label{th:2xndend}
Say the path enters an empty subgrid $s$ whose adjacent cells are also all empty, and where:
\begin{itemize}
\item for any two squares $A$ and $B$ from $\adj(s)$ where there is a path from $A$ to $B$, the shortest such path and the shortest such path without entering $s$ are the same length.
\end{itemize}

Then the path ends there.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The path cannot escape because instead of entering and exiting the empty subgrid, the path could have gone directly to any escape square.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
This applies to when an empty edge of the grid is only adjacent to empty squares.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}
If the path must optimally fill a rectangular empty region which includes an entire edge,
at least one cell of the path must enter the edge, so the path ends there.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}
If this applies to non-intersecting edges, that is, opposite edges of the grid are both at least inside $2 \times n$ empty subgrids, the two ends of the path are within those two edges.
\end{corollary}

In other words, in a \hyperref[def:3814lc]{``latter construction"}, the two ends of the path are within those opposing edges.

Now consider \cref{fig:3x8p1}. We know the path ends within the edges, so with only one more
cell that can be part of a pentomino, there are at least 3 extra empty squares in the middle.

\begin{definition}
First, the \newterm{contangle} of each pentomino.\authornote{Naming is hard}
This is the smallest rectangle that contains the pentomino.
``Contangle" is short for ``containing rectangle".
\end{definition}

\begin{property}
We can simulate the pentomino as its contangle, with the exception of when the path
goes inside it. For \pentI{} this never happens.
\end{property}

\begin{property}
Each contangle is then associated with a ``required empty area".
The idea is that no matter how we place the contangle, the $4 \times 6$ is big enough
that an entire rectangle will be empty.
\end{property}

\begin{property}
However, some of the loss can be ``saved" as long as the path enters the contangle.\marginnote{Breaking the \badterm{assumption}.}

As far as the middle of the path goes, it is useless to enter the contangle if
not entering is just as efficient...\ \emph{or better} than moving along the platter's border.

The proof is as follows:

If the middle of the path enters the contangle, it enters and exits the contangle. The relevant part of the path starts and ends \emph{outside} of the contangle.\authornote{A path has two ends in this paper, not a start and end. But sure.}

Intuitively, we can't move through the platter, so the most efficient path goes barely around it. So moving along the contangle's border is perfectly fine.

Therefore the path must \highlight{end} within the contangle to save squares \textcolor{white!50!authorcolor}{from emptiness}.
\end{property}

\subsection{Inefficient results}

Looking at \cref{tab:single pentomino results}, it remains to show that one pentomino is too few pentominoes for larger grid sizes.

While we could just continue like normal, when it gets to two, three, and more pentominoes,
it seems that something more powerful is needed, otherwise we're going to be stuck
bruteforcing everything manually, which is error-prone.

Consider the temporary function $C$, which takes a pentomino and outputs how much the grid's path would shorten if the pentomino was gone:
\[
C(f) = \#^p_{n,m} - \#^{p \setminus {f}}_{n,m}
\]

We will assign a \newterm{contribution} to each pentomino in the set of placements $p$ - this assignment is not currently even defined or uniquely determined, but we know such an assignment of contribution only makes sense when the sum of contributions for each pentomino is the length of the path:
\[
\left[ \sum_{f \in p} f_{\mathrm{contribution}} \right] = \#^p_{n,m}
\]

Motivated by \crefrange{fig:redundantcontribution}{fig:equalcontribute} we start with a basic precedence algorithm:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
First, the pentominoes where $C(f) = 0$, that is, they don't do anything to the path's length when removed or added back. Say they exist. We will arbitrarily assign one of them a contribution of \highlight{zero} and we \emph{remove} that pentomino from the grid. Then, if there are still pentominoes where $C(f) = 0$, we repeat the process until there are no more such pentominoes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Note that $C(f)$ takes $p$ from context. In context, $p$ is changing, so $C(f)$ may change too even if some pentomino $f$ remains in place.
\end{enumerate}
\item Next, we can remove pentominoes which do not cause any other pentomino $g$ to have $C(g) = 0$. The contribution of a removed pentomino $r$ shall be $C(r)$ at the moment they are removed. By removing pentominoes one by one, we ensure that
\[
\left[ \sum_{f \in p} f_{\mathrm{contribution}} \right] = \#^p_{n,m}
\]
is indeed true.
\item If the above heuristics fail, remove a random pentomino.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bounded aid}
A pentomino's \newterm{contribution} is informally how much longer it makes the path --- such that the sum of the contributions of each individual pentomino is equal to $\#^p_{n, m} - \#^{\emptyset}_{n, m}$. However, there are some difficulties in defining this, as shown by \cref{fig:redundantcontribution,fig:nontrivialcontribution}. For now, the contribution is calculated by placing down an arbitrary sequence of pentominoes such that each pentomino placed makes the path longer, then placing down the redundant pentominoes. As such, the contribution depends on the order in which pentominoes are placed down, and is simply how much the path got longer when the pentomino was placed. Note that the path being made longer includes the possibility that the new path is completely different.

\begin{lemma}
The contribution of a platter $p$, given that the path starts and ends outside the platter, is upper bounded by $a - t$ where $a$ and $t$ are described below. In particular, since $a \le \#(\border(p))$ and $3 \le t$, $\contribution(p) \le \max(\#(\border(p)) - 3, 0)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let the original path be obstructed by a platter (if not, the contribution is zero so it is true thanks to the $\max$).

More specifically, the original path enters the platter for a first time and some time later, exits it for the last time.\footnote{The arrow diagram does not include the possibility that $a$ and/or $b$ are on the border, or that the ``through platter'' sub-path may include multiple exits and entrances of the platter, but the logic still holds.}
\[ A \to \border(p) \to \text{through platter} \to \border(p) \to B \]

Instead, we modify the path to go around the platter. Here we go along the border:\marginnote{Following the border is not necessarily the most efficient route --- an example is \pentU{}.}
\[ A \to \border(p) \to \text{along border} \to \border(p) \to B \]

So the contribution becomes the difference between the lengths of the sub-paths that go (through the platter) and (along the border), notated $t$ and $a$ respectively.

$t \ge 3$ since the path includes at least two cells on the border and one cell inside the platter, and $\#(\border(p)) \ge a$ since the path is along the border.
\end{proof}

\begin{note}
If the platter is not on the edge, then its border forms a cycle and $a \le \lfloor\#(\border(p)) / 2\rfloor$, otherwise at least 3 cells of the border that would exist when the platter is not on the edge are now off the edge of the grid. Either way this further decreases the upper bound.
\end{note}

\begin{note}
The new path may not necessarily be related to the path before the platter was placed. An example is if there were two paths of length 5 and 7, and a platter increased the first path by 3.
\end{note}

\badterm{\textbf{Unproven}}
Given a set of placements $s$ and a pentomino $p$, the contribution of the pentomino is upper bounded by $\#^s_{n, m} - \#^{s-p}_{n, m}$.

The maximum such upper bound is $\#(\border(p)) - 1$, however if the pentomino's platter is not adjacent to the wall, then only half of the entire platter's border and therefore the individual pentomino's border is useful (since it's a loop). Part of the reason why the contribution is so hard to describe is that the maximal minimal path's endpoints may change upon the addition of a pentomino.

\section{Style guide}\label{sec:style}

A single sentence includes a citation:

\begin{quote}
We can see that this (\cref{sec:style}).
\end{quote}

A citation that applies to multiple sentences does not belong to the sentence:

\begin{quote}
We can see that this. Therefore that. (\cref{sec:style})
\end{quote}

Similarly, if a quote contains a sentence, it includes the punctuation. Otherwise it doesn't.
\begin{quote}
``Hello."
\end{quote}

A parenthesized sentence by default does not have the period, because you could also argue the sentence contains the parentheses, and it looks better, especially in a paragraph. But once the parentheses contain two or more sentences, the punctuation goes inside.
\begin{quotation}
My world is changing and I miss youuuuu.
(Japanese sentence I do not remember).
My world is changing and I miss youuuuu.
(Insert more Japanese sentences. When you don't know what the lyrics mean, they are like an instrument but the best kind of instrument where you can imagine what the meaning could be. You can do that with regular instruments, but only to some extent. Also, the feeling when you see what the lyrics actually mean is quite something. I can't imagine that this song is anything other than wholesome though.)

--- Sunglow (Yamajet feat. Hiura Masako)
\end{quotation}

In English grammar, the articles are ``the", ``a", and ``an". The use of articles wrt single letter pentominoes like \pentT{} and \pentY{} seem inconsistent, but actually all use of articles should be consistent with English grammar except for with \pentU{}, because the abruptness tickles a part of my mind.
